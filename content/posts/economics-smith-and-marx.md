[Part 7](/posts/economics-before-capitalism/) traced economic systems up to about 1800: sharing bands, temple economies, feudalism, the Islamic world, Song China, India's villages, mercantilism and colonial extraction. Markets, money and risk-sharing finance were all old. What was new around 1800 was a factory system that produced more, faster, than anything before it.

This part is about that system and the two people whose names still frame every argument about it. I'd heard "Smith versus Marx" all my life without knowing what either actually argued, or that they were mostly answering different questions.

As before, I've kept the questions I had along the way and what cleared them up.

## The Industrial Revolution

Starting in Britain around the 1760s, a cluster of inventions changed how things were made. Textile machines like the spinning jenny, the water frame and later the power loom let one worker produce many times more thread and cloth. James Watt's improved steam engine, from the 1770s, could drive machines anywhere, not just beside rivers. Cheap coal fueled the engines, and better iron made the machines.

Work moved from homes and small workshops into **factories**, where hundreds of workers ran machines owned by someone else. For the first time, private ownership of the means of production, wage labor, market prices, profit and loss, free entry and capital markets all worked together at scale.

{{< stuck "Why did it start in Britain?" >}}
Around 1750, several places were advanced and commercial: parts of China, India, the Netherlands, France. Historians give several reasons for Britain, and argue about how much each mattered.

**High wages plus cheap coal.** A machine is only worth buying if it saves more than it costs. In Britain, wages were high compared with most of the world, and coal was very cheap. Say a spinning machine costs £100 and replaces three workers:

| | Britain | Low wages, expensive fuel |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Yearly wages saved | 3 × £20 = £60 | 3 × £6 = £18 |
| Yearly fuel cost | £5 | £15 |
| Net saving per year | £55 | £3 |
| Worth buying? | Yes: pays for itself in under 2 years | No: would take over 30 years |

Same invention, same engineers, but it only made business sense in Britain. That's the argument associated with the historian Robert Allen.

**Institutions.** Relatively secure property rights, patents that let inventors profit, and growing banks to fund new factories.

**Colonies and trade.** Raw cotton, much of it grown by enslaved people in the American South, and markets to sell finished cloth into. How much this mattered is one of the most heated parts of the debate.

**A tinkering culture.** Lots of practical engineers and craftsmen, connected to scientific ideas, improving machines step by step.

Most historians think it took several of these together.
{{< /stuck >}}

**What it was like.** Early on, it was often brutal: 12 to 14 hour days, dangerous machines, crowded and filthy cities, children working in mills and mines. Historians generally find that British workers' real wages grew slowly or barely at all from about 1780 to the 1830s, even as output soared. Most of the early gains went to owners. After that, wages began rising steadily. The lag is sometimes called **Engels' pause**, after Friedrich Engels, who described the misery of Manchester's workers in 1845. A new technology can raise output for decades before most workers share in it.

It hurt other places too. Cheap machine-made British cloth, helped by colonial trade policy, wiped out much of India's handloom textile industry. India went from a leading exporter of cloth to an exporter of raw cotton for British mills.

**The classical economists.** Thomas Malthus argued in 1798 that population grows faster than food, so wages would always be pushed back down to bare survival. For most of history before then, he was roughly right. He was wrong about what came next, because productivity outran population. David Ricardo showed in 1817 that even if England were worse than Portugal at making both wine and cloth, both would gain if each specialized in what it was *relatively* better at and traded: **comparative advantage**. He also argued that landowners collect rising rents just for owning land as a country grows. These ideas won a major political battle in 1846, when Britain repealed its **Corn Laws**, tariffs that kept grain prices high for landowners, and moved toward free trade.

**The first critics.** People pushed back long before Marx.

- **The Luddites (1811 to 1816)** were skilled hand-weavers and knitters in northern England who saw machines run by cheaper, less-skilled workers cut their pay and wipe out their jobs. They broke into workshops at night and smashed the machines, and the government sent soldiers and made machine-breaking punishable by death. "Luddite" now means someone who hates technology, but their complaint was narrower: the machines were being used to push down their wages and replace them, while the gains went entirely to owners.
- **Robert Owen**, a factory owner, ran his mills at New Lanark in Scotland with shorter hours, schooling for children and decent housing, and still made a good profit. His cooperative community, **New Harmony** in Indiana, founded in 1825, broke apart within a few years over disagreements and a shortage of people with practical skills.
- **Charles Fourier** imagined society reorganized into planned communities of about 1,600 people where work would match people's interests. **Henri de Saint-Simon** imagined a society run by scientists and engineers. Marx later called these thinkers **utopian socialists**: good criticism of capitalism, no realistic theory of how society changes.
- **Workers organized.** Bans on unions in Britain were lifted in 1824, though unions stayed restricted for decades. The **Chartists**, from the late 1830s, demanded the vote for working men. The **Factory Acts**, starting in 1833, banned children under 9 from textile mills and limited hours for older children.

These are four responses to the same problem, bargaining power. The Luddites resisted the technology, Owen tried to be a better owner, the utopians imagined new systems, and the unions and Chartists tried to change the balance of power. That last approach mostly shaped what came next.

## Adam Smith's Argument

Smith wrote two major books. *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759) came first, and it's about ethics. Its central idea is **sympathy**: humans naturally care about others and want their approval. Smith didn't think people are purely selfish, which matters for reading his second book, *The Wealth of Nations* (1776). Its argument, in steps:

1. **A nation's wealth is what it can produce**, not its gold.
2. **Productivity grows when people specialize.** In his pin factory, one untrained worker alone might make one pin a day, and certainly not twenty. Ten workers splitting the job into about 18 steps made upward of 48,000 a day.
3. **Specialization needs trade, and the size of the market limits it.** If Leo only teaches, he has to trade his teaching for food and rent. A village can't support a full-time pin maker; a city or a global market can.
4. **People mostly act in their own interest** in economic life. "It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest."
5. **Competition turns self-interest into serving others.** To earn Sam's fees, a family has to be satisfied. If Sam charges too much or teaches badly, families go to a rival.
6. **Prices guide resources to where they're most wanted.** Every good has a **natural price**, what it costs to produce including normal wages, rent and profit. Shortages push the market price above it and surpluses below it, but competition keeps pulling it back. When tutoring is scarce, prices and profits rise, drawing in new centers until profits fall back to normal.

His conclusions: markets with competition create wealth without anyone directing them, the idea that became known as the **invisible hand**, though the phrase appears only once in the book; government should provide defense, justice, contracts, public works and education, but not grant monopolies or restrict trade; and as wealth grows, wages rise.

**What Smith was not.** He's often treated as the patron saint of leaving everything to the market, but:

- **He distrusted business owners.** "People of the same trade seldom meet together, even for merriment and diversion, but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the public, or in some contrivance to raise prices."
- **He saw real roles for government**, including roads and canals that private businesses wouldn't build, and public schooling for ordinary workers.
- **He worried about the division of labor itself.** Doing one simple task all day, he warned, could leave a worker mentally stunted. That's surprisingly close to what Marx later called alienation.

**The critiques of Smith**

| Critique | Who | The point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Voluntary" isn't really voluntary | Marx | A worker who owns nothing must work or starve. Competition among owners doesn't make the split fair. |
| Competition doesn't last | Smith himself; later antitrust thinkers | Firms tend to merge, collude and become monopolies, and then the invisible hand stops working |
| Prices don't include all costs | Arthur Pigou (1920) | Pollution and other harms nobody pays for get overproduced |
| The whole economy doesn't self-correct | Keynes (1936) | A market for one good settles, but an entire economy can get stuck with mass unemployment for years |
| Buyers and sellers don't know the same things | George Akerlof (1970) | When sellers know more, as with used cars, good products get driven out and markets break down |
| Efficient isn't the same as fair | Many | Markets reward whoever has skills, property and luck to start with, and say nothing about whether that starting point was just, including wealth built on slavery or colonialism |
| People aren't perfectly rational | Behavioral economists like Daniel Kahneman | People misjudge risk, act on habit and get swept up in bubbles |

Most of these don't throw Smith out. They mark the **conditions** under which his argument holds: real competition, honest information, prices that include all the costs, a stable economy, and a fair starting point.

## The Labor Theory of Value

The idea: **the value of a good depends on how much work it takes to make it.**

Smith's example: among hunters, if killing a beaver usually takes twice as long as killing a deer, one beaver should trade for two deer. If beaver traded for less, hunters would switch to deer until the ratio settled back at about two to one. Ricardo extended it to tools: if a fishing net took a week to make, part of that week's labor is in every fish the net catches. Marx made it the center of his theory, using **socially necessary labor time**: the time an average worker, with normal tools, needs to make something society wants. A slow worker doesn't make a shirt more valuable by taking longer.

It seemed right because over the long run, competition does push prices toward the cost of making things, and in the 1700s and 1800s most of that cost was labor.

In the 1870s, three economists, William Stanley Jevons, Carl Menger and Léon Walras, independently replaced it with a different idea: **value comes from what the next unit is worth to people.** Water is essential and takes effort to supply, yet it's cheap because there's plenty. Diamonds are useless and expensive because they're rare. This is the **marginal theory of value**, and mainstream economics has used it since. Labor affects value mainly through supply: things that take more work to make are scarcer.

{{< stuck "Isn't the usual critique of the labor theory a strawman? Marx didn't mean any labor creates value, and at the margin, the value of the next unit does depend on the specific labor that makes it." >}}
Yes, the usual critique was partly a strawman, and the relative version is much stronger. Marx's socially necessary labor time already excludes wasted effort, so "spending a week making mud pies doesn't make them valuable" mostly misses his theory.

And in a competitive market, the marginal view says much the same thing at the margin: prices tend toward the cost of making one more unit, and wages tend toward what the last worker adds to output, the **marginal product of labor**. So output's value and labor's value line up at the margin. That's exactly why the labor theory seemed right to Smith, Ricardo and Marx.

Where the two still split is **which way the arrow points**:

- **Labor theory:** the labor determines the value.
- **Marginal theory:** what people value in the output determines what the labor is worth. Economists say the demand for labor is **derived** from the demand for what it produces.

You can tell them apart when only one side changes. When an AI tutor arrives, an hour of Leo's tutoring takes exactly the same work as before, but its price drops from $100 to about $81 because families now have an alternative. Same labor, lower value. They also split on other inputs: Leo teaches using Sam's classroom and laptops, and crediting all the value to labor is exactly the step that creates "surplus value." And on things that can't be produced at all, like Manhattan land or a famous painting, whose value comes from scarcity with no marginal labor.

The relative framing does lead somewhere important. In 1933, the economist **Joan Robinson** defined exploitation in marginal terms: **paying a worker less than the value of what they add.** That happens when employers have bargaining power, for example when Sam is the only tutoring employer in town, which economists call a **monopsony**. Then Leo might add $900 and still be paid $600, because he has nowhere else to go. The question becomes whether this specific worker is paid what their specific labor adds, and the answer depends on bargaining power. That's where Marx's insight survives inside mainstream economics.

## Marx's Theory of History

Karl Marx (1818 to 1883) believed the most important fact about any society is **how it produces things**. He split that into the **forces of production**, the tools, technology and skills a society has, and the **relations of production**, who owns those tools and who has to work for whom. Together they form a **mode of production**, and he argued that everything else, a society's classes, laws, politics and even its ideas of what's fair, grows out of it. This view is called **historical materialism**.

In each system, one class owns the means of production, another does the work, and the owners take part of what the workers produce:

| System | Who owns the means of production | Who works | How the owners take a share |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Early tribal societies | Nobody: shared | Everyone | They don't. Little is produced beyond survival. |
| Slavery, like ancient Rome | Masters own land and people | Slaves | Openly: slaves get only what keeps them alive |
| Feudalism | Lords own the land | Serfs, tied to the land | Openly: part of the crop, or work on the lord's fields |
| Capitalism | Capitalists own factories and companies | Workers, legally free | Hidden: workers are paid wages, but less than the value they produce |

What moves history from one system to the next: technology keeps improving, the old ownership setup eventually holds it back, and the class that benefits from the new technology overthrows the old ruling class. Under feudalism, merchants and early factory owners grew rich from trade and manufacturing, but lords controlled the land, serfs couldn't leave to work in factories, and guilds restricted production. So this new class, the **bourgeoisie**, overthrew the old order, with the French Revolution as the classic case.

{{< stuck "Aren't slavery, feudalism and capitalism all the same thing?" >}}
That's essentially Marx's argument. They share one structure: one group owns the means of production, another does most of the work, and the owners end up with part of what the workers produce. But they differ in ways that matter:

| | Slavery | Feudalism | Capitalism |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Is the worker owned? | Yes | No, but bound to a lord's land | No |
| Can the worker leave? | No | No | Yes |
| How the owner's share is taken | By force | By law and custom | By a wage contract both sides agree to |
| Is your position fixed at birth? | Usually | Largely | No, though starting wealth matters a lot |
| Who can own the means of production? | Only free people | Mostly nobles and the Church | Anyone who can buy them |
| Pressure to improve production | Weak: slaves gain nothing | Weak: serfs keep little | Strong: profit and competition push constant innovation |

That last row shows up in history. Under slavery and feudalism, living standards barely changed for thousands of years. After industrial capitalism took hold, output per person grew faster than in all of prior history. Marx himself called capitalism the most productive system humanity had built.

Marx's reply is that capitalism swapped legal force for economic force. A serf couldn't leave by law. A worker can leave, but has to work for *someone* or go hungry. He called workers "free in a double sense": free to sell their labor, and free of any property that would let them live without selling it. The defenders' reply is that being able to quit, choose an employer, bargain, change careers or start a business is a difference in kind, and real wages rising enormously backs that up.

Where they meet is the worker's **outside options**. If Leo has savings, in-demand skills and plenty of other employers, voluntary exchange is real: he can walk away, so Sam has to pay him close to what he adds. If he has no savings, one possible employer and no safety net, the choice is closer to "these terms or nothing," and Marx's point gets much stronger. That's why mixed economies invest in exactly what strengthens outside options: education, unemployment insurance, competition between employers. And it's why AI matters here: if machines shrink workers' outside options, Marx's critique gets sharper.
{{< /stuck >}}

## Marx's Argument About Capitalism

1. **Under capitalism, workers own nothing but their ability to work.** Leo has no classroom, so he must sell his time to someone who does.
2. **Labor is the source of value.**
3. **Workers produce more value than they're paid.** The gap is **surplus value**, and it's where profit comes from. Marx called this **exploitation**, in a technical sense rather than a claim that Sam is cruel: the owner keeps value the worker produced.

Per tutor, per month:

| | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Tutoring Leo delivers: 10 students × $100 | $1,000 |
| Books and supplies used up | −$100 |
| **Value Leo's work adds** | **$900** |
| Leo's wage | −$600 |
| **Surplus value kept by Sam** | **$300** |

With two tutors, that's Sam's $600 monthly profit, and in Marx's view, all of it is value the tutors created and didn't keep.

4. **Competition forces owners to keep reinvesting and mechanizing.** If Sam doesn't buy AI tutoring software and cut staff, a rival will, and undercut her.
5. **That squeezes profit over time.** If only labor creates surplus value, replacing workers with machines shrinks the source of profit. Marx predicted a **tendency for the rate of profit to fall**.
6. **Wages stay low.** Machines create a pool of unemployed people, his **reserve army of labor**, so workers can always be replaced and can't bargain up.
7. **Crises keep coming, and big firms swallow small ones.**
8. **Work becomes alienating.** Workers have no say over what they make or how, and don't own the result, so work stops being a way of expressing themselves.

His conclusion: capitalism creates the conditions for its own end.

{{< stuck "Why would workers not being able to buy everything they make cause crises and bigger firms?" >}}
Wages are both a cost and someone's spending. For Sam, Leo's $600 is a cost to keep low. Across the whole economy, wages are most of the money people have to buy things with. Every owner tries to keep wages down, which is sensible for each one. But then workers' total pay is less than the value of what they produce. In the tutoring example, two tutors produce $1,800 of value and are paid $1,200, so workers as a whole can't buy back everything they make.

Someone else has to buy the rest: owners, spending profits on their own consumption or on new investment. In a boom they invest heavily, and factories expand. Eventually there's more capacity than buyers. Goods go unsold, prices and profits fall, owners stop investing, workers are laid off and spend even less. That's a crisis: one person's spending is another's income, running in reverse.

In a crisis, smaller and weaker firms run out of cash first. Bigger ones survive and buy their equipment and customers cheaply, and they produce more cheaply per unit in normal times too. So after each crisis, ownership is concentrated in fewer hands, which Marx called the **centralization of capital**.

Keynes later made a related argument, that slumps happen when total spending falls short, though he blamed investment and confidence rather than low wages. Concentration is real in many industries today, but new firms also keep appearing and displacing old ones, so the picture is mixed.
{{< /stuck >}}

**The mainstream answer about Sam's $600.** Most economists today use a different account. Leo couldn't produce $1,000 of tutoring without the classroom, the books, a business that attracts families, and someone organizing it all. Sam's profit pays for what she contributes:

| Part of Sam's $600 a month | What it pays for | Roughly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interest on the capital | The $5,000 tied up in the center could earn interest elsewhere | $35 |
| Her own work | Running the center, finding families, managing tutors | $400, what she'd earn as a manager elsewhere |
| Reward for risk | If the center failed she'd lose her money; tutors get paid either way | $165 |

And competition pushes wages up: if Leo can earn more at a private school, Sam has to match it or lose him.

{{< stuck "So is there exploitation or not, when the market is competitive?" >}}
It depends on whose definition. With many centers competing for tutors, and the third tutor adding less because of diminishing returns:

| Tutor | Extra students | Value they add |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st | 10 | $900 |
| 2nd | 10 | $900 |
| 3rd | 8 | $750 |

If a tutor adds $750 and is paid $600, a rival can offer $650, then $700, and still profit by hiring them. The bidding stops around **$750**, what the last tutor adds, and since tutors doing the same job get the same wage, all three are paid $750:

| Tutor | Adds | Paid | Kept by Sam |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1st | $900 | $750 | $150 |
| 2nd | $900 | $750 | $150 |
| 3rd | $750 | $750 | $0 |

**The marginal view:** nobody is exploited. Each tutor gets what the market would pay them anywhere. The first two add more only because they have more room, attention and equipment per person, so the $300 is the classroom's contribution: the return on Sam's capital, risk and organizing.

**Marx's view:** the $300 is surplus value. A classroom without tutors is an empty room, so every dollar came from the tutors' work, and Sam keeps $300 only because she owns the room. That's exploitation, even with perfect competition.

| | Marginal theory | Marx |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where the $300 comes from | The classroom's contribution | The tutors' labor |
| Is it exploitation? | No: each input is paid what it adds | Yes: owners take value workers created |
| When is it exploitation? | Only when a worker is paid less than what they add, as with a single employer | Whenever profit comes from others' labor |

So the disagreement underneath everything is whether capital itself produces value, or only lets labor produce it. It gets much sharper with AI, because an AI system is capital that can do some of the work by itself.
{{< /stuck >}}

**The critiques of Marx**

| Critique | Who | The point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The labor theory of value | The marginal revolution (1870s) | Value is set by what people want at the margin, and demand can change prices with no change in labor |
| The numbers don't add up | Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk (1896) | Marx's labor values couldn't be consistently turned into actual prices, the **transformation problem** |
| Profit isn't just surplus value | Böhm-Bawerk; mainstream economics | It also pays for waiting, since money now is worth more than money later, for risk, since owners lose first, and for organizing |
| The predictions failed | The historical record | Real wages in capitalist countries rose for over a century, a large middle class grew, revolutions came in farming countries like Russia and China rather than industrial ones, and capitalism adapted instead of collapsing |
| Socialism can't calculate | Ludwig von Mises (1920), Friedrich Hayek | Without market prices, planners can't know what's worth producing |
| Economic power becomes political power | Hayek, *The Road to Serfdom* (1944) | If the state owns everything, it controls everyone's livelihood |
| The states built in his name | The historical record | The Soviet Union, Mao's China and others became repressive one-party states with famines and labor camps. Marx's defenders reply that these weren't what he described. |

**What Marx got right**, by the account of many economists who reject his theory: bargaining power decides how value gets split, not just fairness; capitalism constantly reinvents how things are made, and Marx described that power more vividly than almost anyone; booms and busts are built in rather than accidental; big firms do tend to get bigger in many industries; and who owns the machines matters, especially when machines replace workers.

## How Marx Thought Socialism Would Come About

1. **Capitalism has to come first.** Socialism needed what capitalism creates: factories, machines, railways, huge productive capacity and a large industrial working class. So Marx expected it in the most advanced countries, like Britain and Germany.
2. **A contradiction builds up.** Production becomes deeply social: thousands of workers depend on each other and on suppliers across the world. But ownership stays private. He argued the gap keeps widening until private ownership holds production back through crises, unsold goods and idle factories, as feudal rules once held back early capitalism.
3. **Society splits into two big classes.** Crises wipe out small firms and big ones absorb them, so fewer people own the means of production. Shopkeepers, craftsmen and small farmers get squeezed into wage work.
4. **Workers become aware of their position.** Marx distinguished between workers sharing a situation and workers *acting* as a class, organized and aware of shared interests. He thought they'd get there through strikes, unions and parties, with crises speeding it up.
5. **Workers take political power.** Often, he expected, by revolution, since owners would resist. But in an 1872 speech he said that in countries with democratic institutions, like England, the US and the Netherlands, workers might win power peacefully. It would be international: "Workers of the world, unite!"
6. **A transitional workers' state.** Workers would run the state for a period, to defend the revolution and reorganize the economy: his "dictatorship of the proletariat," meaning rule by the working class as a whole, not by one dictator. His model was the **Paris Commune** of 1871, whose officials were elected, could be recalled at any time and were paid ordinary workers' wages.

The *Communist Manifesto* (1848) listed first measures for advanced countries. Several are now normal in capitalist ones:

| Demand in the Manifesto | Where things ended up |
| --- | --- |
| A heavily progressive income tax | Adopted in most capitalist countries |
| Credit centralized in a national bank | Central banks, though not monopolies on lending |
| Free education for all children, and an end to child factory labor | Adopted almost everywhere |
| State control of transport and communication | Common for railways and post in many countries, less so today |
| Abolition of private property in land | Not adopted outside socialist states |
| Abolition of inheritance | Not adopted, though most countries tax it |

**What actually happened.** The revolution didn't come where Marx expected. Workers in Britain and Germany won the vote, formed unions and saw wages rise. It came instead in Russia in 1917, a mostly peasant country, and his followers split. **Lenin** argued workers left alone would only fight for better wages, so they needed a **vanguard party** of professional revolutionaries to lead them, and that rich countries avoided revolution by exploiting colonies. **Eduard Bernstein** argued the opposite: capitalism wasn't collapsing, so socialists should win power through elections and gradual reform, which became **social democracy**, the root of today's welfare states. **Rosa Luxemburg** argued against Bernstein that reforms alone could never end capitalism, but also warned that Lenin's vanguard party could become a dictatorship *over* workers rather than *of* them, a warning that proved accurate. **Antonio Gramsci** asked why workers in rich countries didn't revolt at all, and answered that the ruling class shapes culture, schooling and common sense so thoroughly that its rule feels natural: **hegemony**.

So Marx's path split into two roads: revolution led by a party, which produced the Soviet model, and reform through democracy, which produced the mixed economies.

## Socialism and Communism

Marx described two stages after capitalism, most clearly in an 1875 text, *Critique of the Gotha Programme*:

| | Socialism, the first stage | Communism, the final stage |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who owns the means of production | Society: the state or the workers | Society, with no private property at all |
| Is there still scarcity? | Yes | No: abundance is the precondition |
| Is there a state? | Yes, a workers' state | No: it has withered away |
| Are there classes? | Disappearing | None |
| How you're paid | **According to your contribution** | **According to your need** |
| Money and wages | Still exist in some form | Gone |

Under socialism, Sam's center belongs to the workers or the town, Leo is paid by the hours he teaches, and no owner takes a profit, but harder or longer work still earns more. Under communism, tutoring is free to anyone who wants it, and Leo teaches because he enjoys it and takes whatever he needs from what society produces. The core difference is the payment rule: socialism still ties what you get to what you do, and communism cuts that link. Its slogan is **"from each according to their ability, to each according to their needs."**

The words also get used two other ways, which is why they're confusing. The Soviet Union, Mao's China and others were run by **Communist Parties**, because communism was their final goal, but they described their economies as **socialist**, the first stage. The USSR was the Union of Soviet *Socialist* Republics. No country ever reached Marx's final stage. And in everyday politics today, "socialism" often means a capitalist economy with a large welfare state, strong unions and public healthcare, like the Nordic countries. Strictly, that's **social democracy**.

{{< stuck "Isn't socialism just cooperatives?" >}}
A cooperative is one way socialism could be organized, but not the same thing.

| | One worker cooperative | Socialism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scope | One firm, usually inside a capitalist economy | The whole economy |
| Who owns it | That firm's own workers | Society as a whole, or in one version each firm's own workers |
| Where the profit goes | Split among that firm's members | Shared across society, after paying workers for their contribution |
| Coordination | Markets | Markets or a plan, depending on the version |

A single cooperative inside capitalism, like Mondragon in Spain, is an island of worker ownership. An economy made mostly of cooperatives competing in markets is **market socialism**, which Yugoslavia tried. An economy where the state owns everything is **state socialism**, where Leo would be a state employee on a wage.

The key difference is who shares the surplus. In a cooperative, Leo shares the profit of his own center only, so members of a well-located center do well and members of a struggling one do badly. Under socialism in Marx's sense, the surplus goes to society as a whole and funds shared things like schools, hospitals and investment, so workers at a lucky center don't keep extra just because of where they happen to work.
{{< /stuck >}}

{{< stuck "Why do the state and pay disappear under communism?" >}}
Both follow from Marx's definitions plus his assumption of abundance.

**The state.** For Marx, the state isn't just "the government." It's the tool one class uses to rule another: police, courts, prisons and armies protecting the owning class's property. Under communism nobody privately owns the means of production, so there are no classes, so there's no class to protect and none to keep down, so the coercive part of the state has nothing left to do. Engels called this the state **"withering away."** Organization doesn't vanish: someone still runs railways and hospitals. Engels described it as moving from the "government of persons" to the "administration of things."

**Pay.** Pay does two jobs in any economy: it **rations** scarce things, deciding who gets the tutoring when there isn't enough, and it **motivates** work. Marx assumed abundance removes rationing, the way nobody pays for air because there's enough of it, and that work becomes "life's prime want," done for its own sake once it's varied, chosen and meaningful. If nothing needs rationing and nobody needs paying to work, money has no job left.

Distribution by need already happens on small scales: a family dinner table, public libraries and parks, open-source software built by people who aren't paid.

The objections: abundance may never come, because wants grow as societies get richer, and some things can't be made abundant at all, like the house on the best beach or a seat at the top university. Economists call these **positional goods**. Someone still has to do the unpleasant jobs. Coordination still needs information, and without prices that's the knowledge problem again. And in every country run by a Communist Party, the state grew instead of withering.

{{< stuck "AI could make abundance possible. Then could every desire be fulfilled?" >}}
AI could make a huge range of things much cheaper, maybe nearly free. But some scarcity doesn't go away with better technology. It just becomes the new center of economics:

1. **Physical limits.** AI needs chips, electricity, land and raw materials. Food, houses and cars still need matter and energy.
2. **Positional goods.** No technology makes everyone first.
3. **Time and attention.** Everyone still has 24 hours.
4. **Things valued because a human did them.** Live music, a teacher who knows you, a nurse holding your hand. If AI does everything else, human time becomes the scarce part.
5. **Desires that conflict.** I want a quiet street; you want to throw parties on it.
6. **Wants grow.** A level of comfort that would have amazed people a century ago feels ordinary now.

Food shows it already happened once. In rich countries, calories went from scarce to abundant, and scarcity moved: today the expensive things are housing, healthcare, education and childcare. Part of that is **Baumol's cost disease**, after William Baumol: when machines make goods cheaper, services that need human time don't get more productive the same way, so they become relatively more expensive. A string quartet takes four people the same time to play as it did in 1800. That's why tutoring like Sam's has gotten pricier over the decades while TVs got cheaper. AI could change that, which is exactly why an AI tutor is interesting.

And someone still owns the AI, the chips and the data centers, so who gets the abundance, on what terms, is still decided by ownership and power. AI could shift scarcity rather than end it.

## Capitalism Changes

By the late 1800s, both economics and capitalism were changing.

- **The marginal revolution (1870s)** replaced the labor theory of value with value at the margin, and **Alfred Marshall's** textbook in 1890 popularized the supply and demand diagrams still taught today.
- **Henry George** argued in *Progress and Poverty* (1879) that land values rise because of everyone's progress, not the landowner's effort, so land, and only land, should be taxed.
- **Monopolies.** In the US, giant companies like Standard Oil and the big railroads came to dominate their industries. The **Sherman Antitrust Act** of 1890 made monopolizing a market illegal.
- **The first welfare state.** In Germany in the 1880s, Chancellor Otto von Bismarck created the first national health insurance, accident insurance and old-age pensions. He wasn't a socialist: he did it partly to win workers away from the socialists. It became a template for the world.
- **Japan's state-led industrialization.** After the Meiji Restoration in 1868, Japan's government built railways, factories and modern banking, then often sold them to private companies: capitalism with the state driving it.

By 1900, real wages in Britain and other industrial countries were clearly rising. Marx's prediction that workers would get steadily poorer was already looking shaky, though the gains came only after decades of hardship, and only after workers organized and governments stepped in.

| What this era teaches | Where it shows up |
| --- | --- |
| Productivity, not gold or more workers, makes nations rich | The Industrial Revolution |
| Gains from new technology can take decades to reach workers | Engels' pause |
| People displaced by machines resist, and with reason | The Luddites |
| Trade lets both sides gain through specialization | Ricardo's comparative advantage |
| Value comes from what people want at the margin | The marginal revolution |
| Markets left alone can produce monopolies and misery | Standard Oil; factory conditions |
| The fixes came from outside the market | Antitrust, factory laws, unions, Bismarck's social insurance |

Capitalism didn't survive the 1800s by staying pure. It survived by absorbing parts of its critics' demands. The parallel to now is hard to miss: a powerful new technology, huge productivity gains, workers worried about being replaced, and an open question about how long it takes, and what policies it takes, for the gains to be widely shared.

## Where Each Still Holds Up

| Smith still holds up on | Marx still holds up on |
| --- | --- |
| Specialization and trade make societies richer | Bargaining power shapes who captures value |
| Prices coordinate millions of decisions nobody could plan | Capitalism is unstable: booms and crashes are built in |
| Competition disciplines businesses, as long as it lasts | Firms tend to grow larger and concentrate |
| Productivity growth is how wages rise in the long run | New technology can replace workers, and the gains can go to owners first |

They're often presented as opposites, but they mostly answer different questions. **Smith asked how wealth gets created. Marx asked who gets it.** That's the difference between creating value and capturing it, and the AI question needs both: will AI make us far more productive, and if machines do the work, who owns them, and what happens to workers' bargaining power?

## What's Next

The factory system produced more than any system before it, and for decades most of the gains went to owners. Smith explained how competitive markets create wealth, and his critics mapped out the conditions his argument needs. Marx explained capitalism as a class system that hides how owners take a share, predicted crises, concentration and revolution, and imagined socialism and then communism beyond it. His predictions about wages failed, but his questions about bargaining power and ownership didn't go away.
